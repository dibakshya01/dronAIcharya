# m4 — Spec before build

## L1 — Why a 10-line spec saves hours

**Concept.** A **spec** is a short, plain statement of *what* you're building and *how you'll know it's done* — written before any code. It sounds like extra work, but it's the opposite. The AI builds fast; if *you* haven't decided what you actually want, it will build the wrong thing fast, and you'll spend hours going "no, not like that." Ten minutes deciding up front saves those hours. The hard part was never typing the code — it's knowing what you want.

**ELI5.** It's like telling a taxi driver your destination before you get in. Without it, the car still drives — just not to where you meant to go.

**Build-task tie-in.** Before we build your app (say, a little habit tracker), we'll write a 10-line spec together: what it does, who it's for, and what "working" looks like. Then every later step has something to aim at.

**Under the hood.** The spec isn't just planning — it becomes *context* you hand the model (remember m2). A model given your clear intent predicts far better code than one left to guess what "nice" means.

**Check.** Learner can say why writing a short spec first saves time instead of wasting it.

## L2 — Acceptance criteria: how you'll *know* it works

**Concept.** **Acceptance criteria** are the concrete, checkable statements of "it works." Not "login should be nice," but "a user can type an email and password, press submit, and land on their dashboard; a wrong password shows an error." Each one is a yes/no test you can actually perform. They turn a fuzzy wish into something you — and the AI — can verify, and they tell you when to *stop* building a feature.

**ELI5.** They're like a packing list before a trip. Each item gets a tick, so you know you didn't forget the toothbrush — instead of a vague feeling that you're "mostly packed."

**Deep dive (optional).** Good criteria describe *observable, user-visible behavior*, often phrased "a user can X and sees Y" (or "given / when / then"). Bonus: these same sentences later become your actual tests (the test phase of the build), so writing them now does double duty.

**Build-task tie-in.** For the tracker we'll write two or three: "I can add a habit and it shows up in the list," "it's still there after I refresh the page." Short, checkable, real.

**Check.** Learner can turn a vague goal into at least one concrete, checkable acceptance criterion.

## L3 — Writing the spec for *your* idea (hands-on)

**Concept.** Now you write yours. A tiny spec has four parts: **(1)** one line — what it is and who it's for; **(2)** three to five things it must *do*; **(3)** two or three acceptance criteria; **(4)** what it will *not* do (yet). That last part — guarding scope — is a feature, not a weakness: a small thing you finish beats a big thing you abandon.

**ELI5.** It's a napkin sketch, not an architect's blueprint. Enough to aim at, not so much that you never start building.

**Build-task tie-in.** You fill in the template for your real idea; dronAIcharya helps sharpen the wording and flags anything too big for a first build, suggesting what to cut to a finishable core.

**Under the hood.** This spec feeds straight into the build as context, and it's also the yardstick that m6's "trust checklist" measures the AI's output against. One short document, used again and again.

**Check (mastery bar for m4).** Learner has written a tiny spec for their own idea with at least one concrete acceptance criterion.
