# m1 — The security mindset & threat modeling

> Lesson content for the `teach` skill. Teach one lesson at a time, at the learner's level, then check. Don't dump the whole file at them. Security is taught to *defend* — every example exists so the learner can recognize and fix a weakness, never to attack a real system.

## L1 — Think like an attacker *and* a defender

**Concept.** Security isn't a feature you bolt on at the end; it's a *property* of how the system is built. To get it, you hold two viewpoints at once. The **defender** asks "how do I make this work correctly for the right people?" The **attacker** asks "how do I make this do something it wasn't meant to, or see something I shouldn't?" Thinking like an attacker isn't about being sneaky — it's about noticing the assumptions your app quietly trusts, because that's exactly where it breaks.

**ELI5.** A locksmith installs the lock (defender). A good locksmith also walks around the house asking "could I get in through that window instead?" (attacker). You need both or you've locked the door and left the window open.

**Deep dive (optional).** Classic security goals are the **CIA triad**: Confidentiality (only the right people can see it), Integrity (nobody can tamper with it unnoticed), Availability (it stays up for real users). Most attacks are really "break one of these three." Keeping the triad in mind turns a vague worry into a specific question.

**Build-task tie-in.** Pick your app. For one feature (say, "view my profile"), ask both questions: what should happen, and what would an attacker try (view *someone else's* profile? send a weird input?). We'll turn those into a real threat model in L3.

**Under the hood.** "Security through obscurity" — hoping nobody finds the hidden thing — is not security. Assume the attacker can read your code and knows how it works; your defenses must hold anyway.

**Check.** Learner can state that security is designed in, not added later, and give one "what would an attacker try?" question about their own app.

## L2 — Assets, threats, and trust boundaries

**Concept.** Three words carry most of threat modeling. An **asset** is something worth protecting (user passwords, private notes, your API key, the app being available at all). A **threat** is a way an asset could be harmed (stolen, altered, deleted, leaked). A **trust boundary** is any line where data crosses from somewhere you control into somewhere you don't, or vice versa — the browser → your server, your server → the database, a third-party API → your code. Attacks happen *at the boundaries*, because that's where trust is granted.

**ELI5.** Assets are your valuables. Threats are the bad things that could happen to them. Trust boundaries are the doorways of your house — you check who comes through a doorway, not who's already sitting in the living room.

**Deep dive (optional).** A handy checklist for "what could go wrong at this boundary" is **STRIDE**: Spoofing (pretending to be someone), Tampering (changing data), Repudiation (denying you did it), Information disclosure (leaking), Denial of service (knocking it over), Elevation of privilege (getting more access than allowed). You don't need to memorize it — it's a prompt to think past "hacker steals password."

**Build-task tie-in.** List your app's assets (3 is plenty), then draw its boundaries: where does data enter from a user? Where does it leave to an outside service? Each arrow across a boundary is a place to validate and authorize.

**Check.** Learner can name an asset, a threat to it, and one trust boundary in their own app.

## L3 — A lightweight threat model for your app

**Concept.** A threat model is just four honest questions, written down: **(1) What are we building? (2) What can go wrong? (3) What are we going to do about it? (4) Did we do a good job?** You don't need a diagram tool or a security team — a half page is a real threat model if it's specific to *your* app. The goal isn't paranoia; it's spending your limited effort on the risks that actually matter for *this* project.

**ELI5.** Before a road trip you ask: where are we going, what could go wrong (flat tire, running out of gas), what do we pack for it (spare, full tank), and did we actually pack it? Same four questions, for your app.

**Build-task tie-in (hands-on).** Write the four questions for your app in `./.dronacharya/` notes or a scratch file. Example for a notes app with logins: (1) a web app where users save private notes; (2) someone reads another user's notes, a weak password gets guessed, the database leaks; (3) authorization checks on every note, password rules + rate limiting, secrets kept out of the repo; (4) we'll verify each as we build the later modules. That document becomes your map for the whole track.

**Under the hood.** Threat modeling is cheapest early and most expensive late — a design flaw found on paper costs a sentence to fix; found in production it costs an incident. This is why OWASP added "Insecure Design" as its own category (first introduced in the 2021 edition; A06 in the 2025 list): some bugs are design decisions, not typos.

**Check (mastery bar for m1).** Learner has written a four-question threat model for their own app and can point to at least one asset, one plausible threat, and one trust boundary in it.
