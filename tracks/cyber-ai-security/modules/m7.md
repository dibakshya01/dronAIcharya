# m7 — AI security II: excessive agency, data disclosure & LLM supply chain

> Teach one lesson at a time, then check. This module walks the rest of the OWASP LLM Top 10 (2025). Keep it concrete and non-alarmist — the aim is recognizing the risk in the learner's own AI feature and naming one defense.

## L1 — Excessive Agency (LLM06)

**Concept.** **Excessive agency** is giving an LLM-based system too much power: too many tools, too much permission, or too much autonomy to act without a check. The danger is that an LLM can be *wrong* (hallucination) or *hijacked* (prompt injection, m6) — so the more it's allowed to do on its own, the bigger the blast radius when it misfires. The risk isn't the model thinking a bad thought; it's the model being *able to act* on one.

**ELI5.** It's the difference between an intern who can *suggest* "let's delete these files" and an intern who has the keys, the admin password, and permission to delete anything without asking. Give the suggesting intern bad information and you lose nothing; give the all-powerful intern bad information and you lose everything.

**Deep dive (optional).** Three dials to turn down: **excessive permissions** (the tool can do more than the task needs — read-write when read-only would do), **excessive functionality** (more tools wired in than the feature requires), and **excessive autonomy** (it executes high-impact actions with no human confirmation). Mitigations are least privilege for tools, minimizing the toolset, and human-in-the-loop for consequential actions — the whole of m8. This is the LLM-world version of the least-privilege and deny-by-default ideas from m3 and m9.

**Build-task tie-in.** If your app gives an AI any tools or actions, list each one and ask "does the feature truly need this much?" A summarizer that's been handed a file-*delete* tool has excessive agency even if it never uses it — the capability is the risk.

**Check.** Learner can define excessive agency and name one mitigation (least privilege, fewer tools, or human approval).

## L2 — Sensitive Information Disclosure (LLM02) & System Prompt Leakage (LLM07)

**Concept.** LLM apps leak in ways traditional apps don't. **Sensitive information disclosure** (LLM02) is the model revealing things it shouldn't — secrets, other users' data, PII, internal details — because that content was in its context and the model will happily include it in an answer. **System prompt leakage** (LLM07) is a specific case: the hidden system prompt (your instructions, and anything unwisely placed in it) getting extracted. The deeper lesson in LLM07: **the system prompt is not a safe place for secrets** — treat anything in it as potentially discoverable.

**ELI5.** The model is a guest who has seen everything on your desk and has no instinct for what's private. Ask the right way and it'll read your diary aloud. And the "secret note" you taped under the desk (the system prompt) isn't as hidden as you think.

**Deep dive (optional).** Defenses: don't put real secrets or credentials in prompts or system prompts (keep them in the environment, m3 — the model should call a tool that uses the key, not see the key); minimize what sensitive data enters the context at all; scrub/limit PII; enforce access control *before* data reaches the model (a user's agent should only ever retrieve that user's rows — this is where m9's row-level security pays off); and don't rely on "I told it to keep this secret" as a control — instructions are not a security boundary against a determined extractor.

**Build-task tie-in.** Check what ends up in your model's context: are any API keys, other users' records, or internal config in there? Move secrets to tools-behind-the-key, and make sure data access is filtered to the current user *before* it's handed to the model, not after.

**Check.** Learner can name one way an LLM app discloses sensitive data and one defense (keep secrets out of prompts, filter data to the user before it reaches the model).

## L3 — LLM supply chain, data poisoning, and the rest at a glance

**Concept.** The remaining LLM Top 10 categories, briefly:
- **LLM03 Supply Chain** — your model, its weights, fine-tunes, plugins, and datasets are a supply chain, just like your code dependencies (m5). A tampered or untrustworthy model or component is a risk you inherit. *Defense:* use trusted model/source providers, verify integrity, track what you depend on.
- **LLM04 Data and Model Poisoning** — attackers corrupt training or fine-tuning data (or a knowledge base the app retrieves from) so the model learns or retrieves something harmful. *Defense:* control and vet what goes into training and into retrieval stores; validate content before it's stored.
- **LLM08 Vector and Embedding Weaknesses** — in retrieval (RAG) systems, the vector database can leak across users or be poisoned with malicious documents (which then become *indirect injection*, m6). *Defense:* partition by user/tenant, control what gets embedded, treat retrieved text as data.
- **LLM09 Misinformation** — the model confidently produces wrong or fabricated output that users trust (the hallucination problem, now a security risk when decisions ride on it). *Defense:* ground answers in verified sources, surface uncertainty, keep a human on consequential calls.
- **LLM10 Unbounded Consumption** — unchecked use runs up huge cost or denial-of-service (runaway loops, giant inputs, abuse). *Defense:* rate-limit, cap input/output size and spend, monitor usage.

**ELI5.** Beyond "someone tricks the model," there's "someone poisons what it learned or looks up," "someone sneaks bad documents into its memory," "it confidently makes things up," and "someone runs your bill through the roof." Different doors, same house.

**Deep dive (optional).** Note how many of these loop back to earlier lessons: supply chain = m5 for models; poisoning and vector weakness feed m6's indirect injection; misinformation = hallucination from Vibecoding m1. There's also an emerging **OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications** (ASI-numbered, released late 2025) focused on *multi-step autonomous agents* — memory/context poisoning, tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse. Its exact numbering is still settling across sources, so treat it as a "watch this space" companion to the stable LLM list rather than something to memorize; the principles (least privilege, human-in-the-loop, data-not-instructions) are the same ones this track teaches.

**Build-task tie-in.** For each category that touches your app (most small apps: maybe LLM03 and LLM10, plus LLM08 if you use retrieval), write one line in your threat model naming your one defense.

**Check (mastery bar for m7).** Learner can define excessive agency with one mitigation, name one way an LLM app discloses sensitive data, and recognize at least one of the LLM supply-chain / poisoning / consumption risks with a defense.
